Tropical Specimen Trees
Silk Floss Tree
Ceiba speciosa Malvaceae
🌍 Non-native✅ Not Invasive✅ Not Toxic
⚡Quick Hits
  • Those fierce conical trunk spines are hypothesized to be relics of defenses against giant ground sloths that vanished 10,000 years ago.
  • The green-tinted bark is rich in chlorophyll and photosynthesizes even after the leaves drop in fall.
  • The silky floss in its seed pods is buoyant and water-resistant — so similar to kapok it substituted in WWII life jackets.
  • One of the most spectacular flowering events in the park — the entire canopy turns pink in fall when completely leafless.
🌍Origin

Native to the subtropical and tropical forests of South America, primarily Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, and Bolivia. In Bolivia it is called toborochi — 'tree of refuge' or 'sheltering tree' in Guaraní — and is the subject of one of that country's most beloved legends.

🔍More Information
  • True kapok, from its close cousin Ceiba pentandra, was used by the U.S. Navy to fill life jackets through both World Wars. The Silk Floss Tree's floss is so similar it has served as a substitute.
  • In younger trees the green-tinted bark photosynthesizes even after the leaves drop — an evolutionary trick to maximize energy capture during the leafless flowering season. Watch for the color shift to gray as the bark matures, as you can see in this specimen.
  • One study of a single suburban specimen in Mexico documented 79 insect species, eight bird species, and a nectar-feeding bat visiting its flowers — making it a keystone food source in any landscape.
  • Its closest relatives include the Kapok tree and the African Baobab — both also growing at this park — making Palma Sola home to three of the most extraordinary trunk architectures in the plant kingdom.
🦋Wildlife Value

In bloom, it's essentially a pop-up wildlife buffet. Its huge pink flowers produce extraordinary amounts of nectar, feeding bees, butterflies, and migrating hummingbirds during fall when many other nectar plants have finished. Birds raid the burst seed pods for silky floss to line their nests, while the broad canopy provides shade and roosting structure year-round.

🔬Reproduction & Identification
Reproduction

Primarily by seed, dispersed by wind on buoyant silky floss released when pods split open. Deciduous — drops leaves in fall before flowering.

Flowers

Large, showy, hibiscus-like pink to pink-purple blooms 4–6 inches across, appearing October through December in Florida — canopy can be completely covered in full bloom.

Fruit

Large football-shaped green capsules up to 8 inches long that split open at maturity to release black seeds embedded in white silky floss.

Trunk

Bottle-shaped, studded with stout conical spines — dramatic on young trees, more worn on mature specimens. Green-barked when young; gray at maturity.

What to Look For

The spiny trunk is unmistakable year-round. Watch for the dramatic fall flowering when the tree drops its leaves entirely, followed by bursting pods releasing clouds of white floss.

🌱Edibility & Toxicity

Seed oils are edible and used for culinary purposes in South America. The silky floss itself is not edible. No toxicity to people or pets — but use caution around the large trunk spines.
