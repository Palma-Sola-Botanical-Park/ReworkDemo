Weeping Bottlebrush
Melaleuca viminalis Myrtaceae
🌍 Non-native⚠️ Invasive Potential✅ Not Toxic
⚡Quick Hits
  • Those showy red bristles are not petals — they are stamens, the pollen-bearing male organs of the flower.
  • Crush a leaf between your fingers for an immediate burst of fresh citrus fragrance — a reliable field ID trick.
  • One of the most productive wildlife plants in the park: hummingbirds, butterflies, and honeybees swarm the blooms.
  • Though sold as a tree, it is biologically a large shrub trained into tree form.
🌍Origin

Native to eastern Australia, primarily along watercourses in Queensland and New South Wales, with a disjunct population in the Kimberley region of Western Australia. It grows naturally in moist habitats such as riverbanks, creek edges, and flood-prone areas.

🔍More Information
  • The colorful display is a pollen delivery system — engineered to dust visiting hummingbirds and bees as they feed, ensuring cross-pollination across the park.
  • Formerly classified as Callistemon viminalis — you may still find it sold under that name at nurseries. The reclassification to Melaleuca reflects modern genetic analysis.
  • UF/IFAS has predicted this species to be invasive and no longer recommends it statewide. In Central Florida (including Manatee County) it is not currently a documented problem, but should be managed to prevent escape into natural areas.
  • The genus Melaleuca includes some of Florida's most damaging invasive species — most notably M. quinquenervia, which has transformed thousands of acres of Everglades habitat. M. viminalis is a different species, but the family resemblance is worth knowing.
🦋Wildlife Value

The nectar-rich red spikes are magnets for Florida wildlife — especially hummingbirds, butterflies including zebra longwings, and honeybees. A high-value nectar source during warm months when many other plants have finished blooming. Small woody seed capsules provide food for birds and small mammals. The dense weeping canopy offers shelter year-round.

🔬Reproduction & Identification
Seeds

Held in small woody cup-like capsules clustered around the stems; can remain on the plant for years.

Flowers

Bright red bottlebrush spikes 3–4 inches long, appearing mainly spring and summer but sometimes year-round.

Fruit

Small gray or brown woody capsules that look like beads on a necklace, persisting long after flowering ends.

Leaves

Narrow, pale green, lance-shaped. Strongly citrus-scented when crushed — the most reliable field identification feature.

What to Look For

The cascading weeping form is distinctive year-round. In bloom, the vivid red spikes are unmistakable. Crush a leaf to confirm with the citrus scent. Look for hummingbirds hovering at the branch tips.

🍵Edibility & Toxicity

The flowers are non-toxic and can be steeped to make a sweet edible tea or syrup. Not considered a food plant in Florida landscapes, but no toxicity to people or pets.
