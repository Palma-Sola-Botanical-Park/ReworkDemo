Culturally & Historically Significant
Queen’s Crepe Myrtle
Lagerstroemia speciosa Lythraceae

🌍 Non-native ✅ Not Invasive ✅ Non-toxic
⚡Quick Hits
  • Its leaves contain corosolic acid, studied for lowering blood sugar; leaf tea is a traditional diabetes remedy in the Philippines and India, where the plant is called Banabá.
  • The timber ranks second only to teak in parts of Asia and has built homes and boats for centuries.
  • Each flower makes two kinds of pollen at once — a generous batch of sterile “dummy” pollen as a bee reward, and a smaller hidden batch of fertile pollen that does the real work.
  • It’s the official state flower of Maharashtra, India.
🌍Origin

Native to tropical South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Melanesia — including India, the Philippines, Malaysia, and northern Australia.

🔍More Information

This is one of the more medicinally significant trees in Asian traditional medicine: its leaves contain corosolic acid, studied in clinical trials for lowering blood sugar in Type II diabetics, and leaf tea is a traditional treatment in the Philippines and India. The wood is very strong — ranked second only to teak in parts of Asia — and has long been used for houses, boats, and railroad ties. It is a fast grower that blooms young, so you don’t wait decades for the show, and its bark peels in gray and tan patches like a sycamore. Each flower carries two kinds of pollen at once: a generous offering of nutritious yellow pollen that exists only to reward visiting bees, and a small hidden batch of fertile blue-green pollen that does the actual reproductive work — the tree feeds the bees while quietly getting pollinated. Maharashtra, India, names it the official state flower, and one of its close relatives, the common crepe myrtle (Lagerstroemia indica), is among the most planted street trees in the American South. In Theravada Buddhism the tree is linked with enlightenment, and in Hindu tradition its flowers are associated with Lord Brahma and taken as a sign of household prosperity. It carries dozens of names across its range.

🦋Wildlife Value

The large flower panicles are a documented pollen source for bees through the hot summer months, when few other sources are available in Florida landscapes. A University of Florida study recorded honeybees, bumblebees, and native carpenter bees foraging on it — the two pollen types may be why it draws such a range of bee sizes. Birds nest in the broad canopy, and the persistent woody capsules feed seed-eating birds through winter.

🔬Reproduction & Identification
Reproduction

By seed and cuttings. Seeds are small and winged, carried on the wind from dry woody capsules.

Flowers

Erect panicles 20 to 40 cm long with large 2- to 3-inch crinkled crepe-paper blooms in pink to lavender, spring through summer and often into fall; a dominant flowering event that can cover the canopy.

Fruit

Dry, hard brown oval capsules 1 to 2 inches long that persist into winter and split to release winged seeds.

Leaves

Large, dark green, oblong and leathery, up to 12 inches long. One of the few deciduous trees in South Florida — leaves turn red or orange before dropping.

Bark

Smooth, mottled gray and tan, peeling in patches — ornamental year-round, like a sycamore.

What to Look For

The flaking patchwork bark stands out in every season. Watch for the summer panicles covering the canopy in pink to lavender, and the red fall color before the leaves drop — one of the few deciduous color shows in a South Florida garden.

✅Edibility & Toxicity

No toxicity to people or pets, and not listed as toxic to dogs (ASPCA). The leaves are used for medicinal tea across Southeast Asia; the fruit is not commonly eaten, and this is not a food plant in Florida landscapes.
