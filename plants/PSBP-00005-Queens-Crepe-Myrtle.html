Culturally & Historically Significant
Queen's Crepe Myrtle
Lagerstroemia speciosa Lythraceae
🌍 Non-native✅ Not Invasive✅ Not Toxic
⚡Quick Hits
  • Leaf tea is a traditional diabetes treatment across the Philippines and India — clinical trials confirm it lowers blood sugar.
  • The wood is ranked second only to teak in strength across parts of Asia, used for homes, boats, and railroad ties for centuries.
  • Each flower runs a clever con: producing showy dummy pollen to reward bees while hiding the real fertile pollen separately.
  • The state flower of Maharashtra, India — and associated with enlightenment in Theravada Buddhist tradition.
🌍Origin

Native to tropical South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Melanesia — including India, the Philippines, Malaysia, and northern Australia. Known across its range by dozens of names, reflecting centuries of cultural significance.

🔍More Information
  • The leaves contain corosolic acid, a compound shown in randomized clinical trials to lower blood glucose levels in Type II diabetics — making leaf tea a genuine traditional medicine, not just folklore.
  • Each flower produces two types of pollen simultaneously: generous nutritious yellow dummy pollen that rewards visiting bees, and a small hidden batch of fertile blue-green pollen that does the actual reproductive work.
  • In Theravada Buddhism, the 11th and 12th Buddhas are said to have achieved enlightenment beneath this tree. In Hindu tradition, the flowers are associated with Lord Brahma and considered a sign of prosperity.
  • One of the few deciduous trees in South Florida — leaves turn red or orange before dropping in winter, offering one of the most dramatic color displays available in a South Florida garden.
🦋Wildlife Value

The large flower panicles are a documented pollen source for bees through the hot summer months when few other sources are available in Florida landscapes. A University of Florida study documented honeybees, bumblebees, and native carpenter bees foraging on this species. The dimorphic pollen — two types simultaneously — may explain why it attracts such a diverse range of bee sizes. Birds use the broad canopy for nesting, and persistent woody seed capsules provide food for seed-eating birds through winter.

🔬Reproduction & Identification
Reproduction

By seed and cuttings. Seeds are small and winged, dispersed by wind from dry woody capsules.

Flowers

Spectacular erect panicles 20–40 cm long bearing large 2–3 inch blooms in pink to lavender with crinkled crepe-paper petals, appearing spring through fall.

Fruit

Dry, hard brown oval capsules 1–2 inches long, persisting on the tree into winter and splitting to release small winged seeds.

Leaves

Large, dark green, oblong and leathery, up to 12 inches long. One of the few deciduous trees in South Florida — turns red or orange before dropping in winter.

Bark

Smooth, mottled gray and tan, peeling in patches — ornamental year-round, similar in character to a sycamore.

What to Look For

The flaking patchwork bark is distinctive in every season. Watch for dramatic summer flower panicles covering the canopy in pink to lavender, and the striking red fall leaf color before leaf drop.

🍵Edibility & Toxicity

Leaves are used to make medicinal tea in Southeast Asia — corosolic acid content has been validated in clinical trials for blood sugar regulation. Fruit is not commonly eaten. No toxicity to people or pets.
