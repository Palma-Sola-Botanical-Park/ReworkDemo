Native Wetland and Pond Edge
Lanceleaf Arrowhead
Sagittaria lancifolia Alismataceae
🌿 Florida Native✅ Not Invasive✅ Not Toxic
⚡Quick Hits
  • The genus name Sagittaria comes from the Latin for arrow — named for the unmistakable arrowhead shape of the leaves. The shape is so distinctive that it has been the identifying feature of this plant since before botanical Latin was invented.
  • The starchy underground tubers — duck potatoes — were a staple food for Indigenous peoples across the southeastern United States and were gathered and traded widely. Ducks and muskrats eat them too, which is where the folk name comes from.
  • An emergent aquatic native — grows with its roots in the water and its foliage above the surface. Stabilizes shallow pond banks by rhizome.
  • The flowers are three-petaled and white with a yellow center — simple, clean, and worth a look up close.
🌍Origin

Native to the southeastern United States and tropical America. Common in Florida freshwater marshes, pond edges, and slow-moving water. Member of Alismataceae — the water-plantain family.

🔍More Information
  • Lanceleaf Arrowhead is one of the more historically significant plants at the water's edge. The tubers — called duck potatoes — were a reliable starchy food source for Indigenous peoples across the Southeast, harvested by wading into shallow water and loosening them from the mud with their feet or toes. The tubers float to the surface once freed. They were eaten roasted or boiled and were traded between communities. European settlers adopted the same practice.
  • The plant is doing active ecological work in the ponds. The rhizome network stabilizes the shallow bank zone, and the emergent foliage creates habitat structure at the water surface used by fish, frogs, aquatic insects, and wading birds. Ducks and muskrats eat the tubers; blackbirds and other waterbirds eat the seeds.
  • The arrowhead leaf shape is one of the more distinctive silhouettes in the Florida wetland plant palette — recognizable at a distance, unambiguous up close.
🦋Wildlife Value

The tubers are eaten by ducks, muskrats, and other wetland mammals. Seeds eaten by blackbirds and waterbirds. The emergent foliage creates surface habitat structure for fish, frogs, aquatic insects, and wading birds foraging at the water edge.

The rhizome network contributes to shallow bank stabilization — relevant to the park's ongoing pond erosion prevention work.

🏷️Also Known As
Also called bulltongue arrowhead and duck potato. Sometimes treated as subspecies or varieties (for example subsp. media). Carries many historical scientific synonymsincluding Sagittaria angustifolia and Sagittaria ovata.
🔬Reproduction & Identification

Reproduction and Identification

Flowers — Three white petals with a yellow center, on branched stalks rising above the foliage. Male and female flowers sometimes on separate stalks on the same plant. Clean and simple up close.

Fruit and Seeds — Rounded heads of small flattened seeds eaten by waterbirds.

Tubers — Starchy underground tubers (duck potatoes) along the rhizomes. Edible — historically roasted or boiled. Eaten by ducks, muskrats, and other wetland wildlife.

Leaves — Strongly arrowhead-shaped (sagittate), on long petioles rising from the water. Very variable in size. The shape is the definitive identification feature.

Form — Emergent aquatic perennial, 2 to 5 feet above water. Spreads by rhizomes to form colonies in shallow water and saturated soil.

What to Look For

The arrowhead leaf shape is unmistakable — no other plant at the pond edge has quite that silhouette. White three-petaled flowers on tall stalks above the foliage when in bloom.

📐Size & Growing Conditions
An emergent aquatic perennial, typically 3 to 5 feet tall (occasionally taller), forming clumps 1 to 3 feet wide. The smooth, lance-shaped leaf blades can reach about 10 inches long.
Wants full sun and shallow standing water or consistently saturated soil — it grows along pond and lake edges, in freshwater and brackish marshes, and at muddy stream margins. It should not be allowed to dry out, and it has little tolerance for direct salt spray. A classic choice for water gardens, rain gardens, and wetland restoration.
🌱Edibility & Toxicity

Edible. Tubers edible — historically eaten roasted or boiled by Indigenous peoples of the southeastern United States. Not a casual trail snack; harvest requires wading.
